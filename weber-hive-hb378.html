CASE 2026-378

H.B. 378 · 2026 General Session

The Dust Rule With No Rules Left

The introduced bill required every dust-producing facility to operate under an approved control plan and imposed a mandatory compliance fee with real enforcement. What became law kept only a public-signage requirement and an optional fee the state isn't required to ever charge.

Chief Sponsor: Rep. Stephanie GriciusSenate Sponsor: Sen. Derrin R. Owens Effective: May 6, 2026 Became Law

New Here? Start Simple

Rock quarries, gravel pits, and big excavation sites can kick up a lot of dust that blows into nearby neighborhoods.

The bill as written required every one of these sites to have an approved plan for controlling that dust before they could even operate — like a permit. If a site broke the rules, the state could send a formal notice, demand a corrective plan, and inspect it up to three times a week for six weeks.

A substitute removed all of that. What became law keeps a sign posted at the entrance with contact information, and a fee the state is allowed to charge — but isn't required to.

What Changed, Section by Section

As Introduced

What Became Law

How This Could Have Actually Protected Nearby Communities

The introduced version had real teeth. A public-benefit version of this bill would have kept them:

  • Kept the requirement that a facility have an approved dust control plan before it can operate — the equivalent of a basic operating permit
  • Made the compliance fee mandatory, not optional — a fee the state is never required to charge doesn't fund any oversight
  • Kept the noncompliance enforcement ladder (notice, corrective action report, increased inspections) so residents near a noncompliant site have some real recourse
  • If cost concerns drove the change, phased the fee in gradually instead of making the entire enforcement structure optional
