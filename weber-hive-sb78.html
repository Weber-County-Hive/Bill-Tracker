CASE 2026-078 · 2026 GENERAL SESSION

S.B. 78 — Property Tax Relief Amendments

Status: Failed · Died in the House

Failed
Mixed · Leaning Harmful
A bill that started out expanding property tax relief ended up, by its final substitute, repealing renter credits and homeowner credits outright — and never got a committee hearing on that final version before dying on the House floor.
Process Red Flag

The version that failed was never heard in committee

By the time S.B. 78 reached its final form, it had been substituted four times. The final substitute — the one that repealed renter and homeowner credits and replaced existing abatements with an optional county program — never received its own committee hearing before going to the floor.

That matters for accountability regardless of how anyone feels about the policy: the public and other legislators were voting on, and reacting to, a version of the bill that never got the normal public vetting process.

Result Failed

The Senate passed an earlier version of the bill; the source record does not report that Senate vote's tally. The final House vote — on the S4 substitute that had never been heard in committee — failed 28–41–6.

The Public-Interest Bottom Line

S.B. 78 is a case study in how much a bill can change between introduction and its final vote. It started as an expansion of renter and homeowner relief and ended, four substitutes later, repealing both — replacing statewide programs with a patchwork of optional county participation, and creating an interest-bearing lien structure in place of straightforward deferrals.

The bill failed, so none of this became law. But the process itself is the story: the version that mattered most — the one lawmakers actually voted on — never got a public committee hearing.

When a bill's substance reverses this much between introduction and final vote, should the final version be required to get its own committee hearing before reaching the floor?
