Utah’s School Book Laws, 2022–2026

From 2022 to 2026, Utah lawmakers tried six bills about which books belong in school libraries. Two became law and four died. Here is who wrote each one, how lawmakers voted, and how each one grades.

Short version

  • From 2022 to 2026, six bills tried to change how Utah schools handle “sensitive material,” the state’s legal name for pornographic or indecent material. Two passed: H.B. 374 (2022) and H.B. 29 (2024), both with Rep. Ken Ivory as primary sponsor (H.B. 374 bill page; H.B. 29 bill page).
  • H.B. 29 is the one that takes a book out of every Utah public school once enough districts take it out. Per KSL, that’s three school districts, or two districts plus five charter schools. Only a State School Board vote can bring it back (KSL).
  • Four later bills died: H.B. 138 (2023, Rep. Melissa Ballard), H.B. 473 (2025) and H.B. 197 (2026), both by Rep. Nicholeen Peck, and H.B. 517 (2026, Ivory). Three of them passed the House first.
  • By Sept. 28, 2026, 44 titles had been removed from every Utah public school under the statewide rule. Every one was triggered by school districts; no charter school is listed on any of them (TownLift, Oct. 1, 2026; KUER, Oct. 9, 2026).
  • A group that includes the ACLU of Utah, authors and two high school students sued in federal court in January 2026, saying H.B. 29 breaks the First Amendment. The state has asked the court to dismiss the case; no ruling on the main question had been reported as of May 2026 (Deseret News; Fox13).
  • Who funds the sponsors: Ivory’s biggest group donors are two House Republican PACs ($35,500 and $33,500). Peck’s biggest is the Tooele County Republican Party ($3,100). The full table, with the State School Board, is on the Education case file.
  • Graded by the collective rubric, the two laws grade B (H.B. 374) and C− (H.B. 29). The lowest grade, D, goes to Ivory’s H.B. 517 (2026), which died: it would have kept state courts from blocking the law and made people who sue to block it pay the other side’s fees.

Where & who

WhereEvery Utah public school district and charter school, statewide.

This isn’t a personal opinion of any bill. Each bill is graded on the same five questions used across the Hive: Who holds the power? Can the public see what happens? Is the money accounted for? What does it do to land and water? What does it do to the community? Grades are based on what the bill’s own words would do. Whether it became law is shown separately.

In plain words: C means it does what the law already requires. B or A means it does more for the public. D means it makes a public check weaker. F means it takes one away with nothing in its place.

The six bills

H.B. 374 (2022): Sensitive Materials in Schools LAW

Primary sponsor: Rep. Ken Ivory · Senate sponsor: Sen. Todd Weiler · Signed March 24, 2022 · Official bill page

In plain words: This made pornographic or indecent books against state rules in public schools, and said parents from each school’s community must help decide which books cross that line.

What it does (from the bill’s own summary):

  • Defines “sensitive materials” and prohibits them in public schools.
  • Has the State School Board, working with the Attorney General’s office, give schools guidance and training on spotting sensitive materials.
  • Requires a school district or charter school to include parents “reflective of a school’s community” when deciding whether a book is sensitive material.
  • Requires the State School Board to report to two legislative interim committees on how schools are complying and on complaints received.

H.B. 138 (2023): Sensitive Material Requirements DIED

Primary sponsor: Rep. Melissa Ballard · Senate sponsor: Sen. Lincoln Fillmore · Died March 3, 2023 (filed without a Senate floor vote) · Official bill page

In plain words: This would have made schools finish every book review they started and tell the state about books that broke the rules. It passed the House but never got a Senate vote.

What it does (from the bill’s own summary):

  • Requires a school district or charter school to finish its review of material that someone says breaks state law, and to report material that does break it to the State School Board.
  • Has the State School Board review statewide curriculum or program materials in some cases.
  • Provides for ending contracts with vendors whose instructional material includes sensitive material.
  • The House-passed version (5th Substitute) carried the title “Digital Instructional Material Requirements.”

H.B. 29 (2024): Sensitive Material Review Amendments LAW

Primary sponsor: Rep. Ken Ivory · Senate sponsor: Sen. Todd Weiler · Signed March 18, 2024 · Official bill page

In plain words: This is the law that takes a book out of every public school in Utah once enough districts take it out. Only the State School Board can vote to bring it back.

What it does (from the bill’s own summary):

  • Says protecting children from illicit pornography comes before other considerations when evaluating instructional material.
  • Names who may start a formal review: per KSL, school employees, students, parents and school board members.
  • Splits material into “objective” and “subjective” sensitive material, with different review steps. Per KSL, district administrators can remove objective material without a review committee.
  • If a set number of districts find a book is objective sensitive material — per KSL, three school districts, or two districts plus five charter schools — it comes out of every Utah public school, unless the State School Board votes to overturn it.
  • Provides indemnification for claims arising from the sensitive-materials requirements. (In plain words: the state agrees to cover claims made against people because they followed these rules.)
  • Requires the Legislative Auditor General to audit school district compliance.

In plain words: This would have given parents more information about the book rules and a place on every district’s website to report a book. It passed the House but never got a Senate vote.

What it does (from the bill’s own summary):

  • Adds sensitive-material information to the State School Board’s parent portal.
  • Requires each district and charter school to give parents certain information at registration every year.
  • Requires a section on each district’s website homepage for reporting sensitive material.
  • Sets rules for sensitive material in digital instructional content, including filters (per the Legiplex bill summary).

H.B. 197 (2026): School Materials Amendments DIED

Primary sponsor: Rep. Nicholeen P. Peck · Senate sponsor: Sen. Keven J. Stratton · Died March 6, 2026 (held in Senate committee) · Official bill page

In plain words: This would have made schools check every new book before adding it, and kept books removed statewide out of every school. It passed the House, then stopped in a Senate committee.

What it does (from the bill’s own summary):

  • Requires a district or charter school to review instructional material before adding it to its collection.
  • Puts prohibitions on vendors of digital instructional material, including selling or promoting goods through that material (per the Better Utah bill tracker).

H.B. 517 (2026): Sensitive Materials DIED

Primary sponsor: Rep. Ken Ivory · Senate sponsor: none listed · Died March 6, 2026 (enacting clause struck; no recorded vote) · Official bill page

In plain words: This would have made the book law harder to challenge in court. People who sued to stop it could have had to pay the other side’s lawyers, and state courts could not block it. It never got a vote.

What it does (from the bill’s own summary):

  • Requires the State School Board and every local board to adopt a policy that school libraries provide “access only to materials that will most advance the education of a student,” and bars adding sensitive material or statewide-removed material. It does not require removing books already on shelves (lines 234–244).
  • Lets a local board ask each student and parent, before the 2027–28 school year, to agree to individual arbitration of claims under this section (lines 245–260). (Arbitration is a private hearing instead of a public court case.)
  • Gives a person harmed by a school’s failure to comply a right to sue the school for an order to comply; no money damages (lines 261–263).
  • Makes anyone who sues to block the law, and their lawyer, “jointly and severally liable for the costs and reasonable attorney fees the prevailing party incurs” (lines 264–284). (In plain words: if they lose, the person who sued and their lawyer could each be made to pay the other side’s whole legal bill.)
  • Keeps the state’s and schools’ immunity against suits challenging the law, says state courts lack jurisdiction to block it, and bars class actions; a person can still argue the law is invalid as a defense (lines 298–324). (Immunity means protection from being sued.)
  • Has the state defend and indemnify people enforcing it, including court costs and attorney fees (lines 222–227). No money is appropriated (line 20).
  • Would have taken effect May 6, 2026, or on signing with a two-thirds vote of each house (lines 338–344).

What happened next

  1. Oct. 14, 2025 State auditors (the Legislative Auditor General) checked 22 middle and high school books from a list of 186 titles. They found 21 of the 22 had sexual content under Utah’s legal definition. Per KSL, a lawmaker the article does not name made and rated the 186-title list (KSL, Oct. 14, 2025). H.B. 29 is the law that requires these compliance audits (H.B. 29 bill page).
  2. Jan. 2026 The ACLU of Utah, authors Elana K. Arnold, Ellen Hopkins and Amy Reed, the estate of Kurt Vonnegut and two high school students sued in federal court, arguing H.B. 29 violates the First Amendment (Deseret News, Jan. 6, 2026).
  3. Feb. 13, 2026 Stephen King’s Bag of Bones became the 23rd title removed from every Utah public school, after removals in Davis, Granite, Jordan and Tooele school districts (KPCW; KUER list; Book Riot).
  4. March 2026 H.B. 197 and H.B. 517 both died at the end of the session (H.B. 197; H.B. 517).
  5. May 1, 2026 KUTV reported that 33 of the 34 books then removed statewide were first removed in Davis School District (KUTV).
  6. May 13, 2026 First hearing in the lawsuit, before U.S. Magistrate Judge Cecilia Romero. She kept the student plaintiffs’ names private. The Attorney General’s Office, representing the State School Board and districts, has asked the court to dismiss the case; no ruling on that was reported (Fox13).
  7. July 13, 2026 The statewide list reached 36 titles (Salt Lake Tribune).
  8. Aug. 6, 2026 The State School Board adopted a library review plan with $60,000 and voted 10–5 to include Board Member Emily Green’s Library Review Addendum (USBE minutes, Aug. 6, 2026, approved Oct. 2026). Full vote names are on the Education case file.
  9. Sept. 28, 2026 Seven more titles added, for 44 in all (TownLift). The full list and the districts behind each title are on the Education case file.

What would make this clearer for the public

These come from gaps in the record, not from either side of the book debate. They would help anyone, for or against removals, see what is happening.

1. Read and post every fiscal noteA fiscal note is the Legislature’s estimate of what a bill costs. Five of these six bills have not had theirs read for this page, so their money grades are blank. Who can help: the Legislature posts them on each bill page; the Hive will add them.
2. Show what the state pays to defend the lawH.B. 29 has the state cover claims against people following it, and H.B. 517 would have paid enforcers’ legal costs with no money set aside. With a federal lawsuit underway, a public total of what defending the law costs would answer the money question. Who could do it: the Attorney General’s Office or the Legislature.
3. One public list with dates and schoolsA running list of every book, every school that removed it and the date would show exactly how each statewide removal was reached. Who could do it: the State School Board.
4. Name who made the 186-title listState auditors worked from a 186-title list that, KSL reports, an unnamed lawmaker made and rated. Who could do it: the Legislature or its auditors.
5. Audit charter schools tooH.B. 29 only requires auditors to check school districts. Charter schools are public schools too. Who could do it: the Legislature, by changing the law.
6. Challenge counts for every districtHow many challenges each district gets, and from how many different people, is not posted by the state. KUTV found most removals start in one district (KUTV). Who could do it: each district, or the Legislature by law.
7. Post the State School Board’s final survey before it goes outThe board’s $60,000 library review memo was adopted “for staff refinement and committee discussion, not as a final form” (USBE minutes). Posting the final questions, and what the money pays for, would let the public see what is being asked. Who could do it: the State School Board.
8. Fix the minutesThe board approved its Aug. 6, 2026 minutes in October, and the approved version still lists one member on both sides of a vote and counts 16 votes on a 15-member board (see the vote table). Who could do it: the State School Board, by correcting them.
9. A clear path to a “put it back” voteUnder H.B. 29, only the State School Board can return a book to every school. We did not find a posted process or schedule for how a book gets that vote, or a record of one ever happening. Who could do it: the State School Board.
10. Say whether districts get paid for the $60,000 reviewThe board’s posted plan counts 789 hours of district staff time but says nothing about payment; an email from a tip says schools taking part “will be compensated” (details). Who could do it: the State School Board.
