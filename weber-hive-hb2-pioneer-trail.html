CASE 2026-HB2-PT

H.B. 2 · 2026 General Session · Pioneer Trail grants

Four Doors to One Trail

The state budget bill sends up to $10 million to the Pioneer Trail Foundation through four separate agencies and four separate pots of money. Only one of the four is matched to new money shown in its own line of the bill. One House member tried to stop it on the floor; the House declined to pause debate for his change.

Primary Sponsor: Rep. Val L. Peterson · Senate Sponsor: Sen. Jerry W. Stevenson Effective: Signed March 26, 2026 Became Law

New Here? Start Simple

"A group had an idea for a walking trail. It formed a nonprofit. The state budget then named that nonprofit for up to $10 million, split four ways."

Every year Utah's Legislature passes budget bills. H.B. 2 is one of them. It is thousands of lines long, and most of it is ordinary: money for courts, colleges, roads and state offices.

Tucked inside are four short paragraphs, in four different parts of the bill, that each send money to the same private nonprofit: the Pioneer Trail Foundation. The foundation is planning a walking route in downtown Salt Lake City linking Temple Square and the State Capitol.

Add the four together and you get up to $10 million. You would not see that total by reading any one line. And only one of the four lines is matched to new money shown in its own item. Two name sources that their items add no H.B. 2 money from, and the fourth sits in an item whose H.B. 2 additions are smaller than the grants written into it. Because H.B. 2 adds to earlier budget bills, that does not mean money is missing; it means the bill alone does not show which dollars pay for the trail.

None of this says the trail is a bad idea. Lawmakers who spoke for it called it a future landmark. This page lays out what the bill says, what the vote record shows, and what it does not.

Each line below is quoted from the enrolled H.B. 2 (2026). Each one calls the payment a "direct award grant" under Utah Code Title 63G, Chapter 6b, and each names the Pioneer Trail Foundation as the recipient.

What "up to" means: three of the four grants are written as caps. The bill allows up to that amount; it does not show what was actually paid. Payment records would show that.

What the state budget site shows: the Legislature's budget site (COBI) lists two funded items for the trail: "The Pioneer Trail," $5,000,000 one-time, and "Pioneer Trail Project," $2,000,000 one-time. That is $7 million. There is no funding item for the other two grants (items 148 and 170), which matches the bill: neither item adds H.B. 2 money from the source its grant names. H.B. 2 adds to amounts appropriated in other budget bills, so these lines show only what changed in H.B. 2, not every dollar available.

Why four sources: the Deseret News reported (April 28, 2026) that the trail was funded from four different sources because of a tight budget year.

What the four paragraphs do and don't say: the four trail grant paragraphs do not state a matching contribution or a report back to lawmakers. In the same bill, four homelessness funding items in item 46 do state both: a 1:1 local match and a report to the Executive Appropriations Committee before spending. However, each trail paragraph invokes Utah's State Grants chapter (Utah Code Title 63G, Chapter 6b), which has its own requirements. The grant agreements and that chapter would have to be reviewed before concluding what budgets, deliverables, reporting or other conditions apply.

Not found by name: we searched a copy of the Legislature's public list of 2026 Requests for Appropriation (copied Oct. 4, 2026) for "Pioneer," "Capitol" and "Council Hall" and found no request under those search terms. A request could exist under another title. News reports say Gov. Spencer Cox's budget asked for $5 million for the trail.

Where the Trail Would Go

The foundation describes a 1.8-mile route from Temple Square to the State Capitol. KSL reported that the loop would link Temple Square, City Creek Park, Council Hall, the Capitol and Memory Grove. The Deseret News described the main line running past Temple Square along South Temple, then north up State Street to the Capitol, with a side route to Brigham Young's pioneer cemetery, the Cathedral of the Madeleine and the First Presbyterian Church. Reported lengths vary (1 mile, 1.8 miles, 2 miles). The markers below show the five landmarks KSL named. They are approximate points for orientation, not the official route, which has not been published in the records reviewed here.

What Happened on the House Floor

On March 3, 2026, H.B. 2 came up for its final House debate at 9:47 a.m. Rep. Nelson T. Abbott, R-Orem, filed House Amendment 1 at 9:59 a.m. It would have deleted all four Pioneer Trail grants. The deleted text is shown in braces { } in the amendment.

According to KSL's report that day, Abbott asked the House to pause debate so he could bring the amendment, saying the trail could be a good thing but questioning whether it was worth $10 million in state money. In his words: "does it deserve $10 million in state money? I don't think so." Rep. Kristen Chevrier, R-Highland, also backed pausing, saying several of her committee's recommendations were not in the budget. Budget chair Rep. Val Peterson responded that the budget had been negotiated with the Senate and that changing it would undo weeks of work. KSL reported that the effort to postpone debate failed.

Speaking to the Deseret News later, Abbott said the project did not get a typical committee hearing or recommendation, that he does not oppose the idea itself, and that he had not seen details of how the money would be used: "Before we pay for the path, we need to see the map."

Two minutes after the amendment's time stamp, at 10:01 a.m., the House passed H.B. 2, 68–6–1, with all four grants still in it.

The Senate passed the bill the next day, March 4, 25–0–4. KSL reported that Senate leaders, including budget chair Sen. Jerry Stevenson and Minority Leader Luz Escamilla, spoke in favor of the trail funding. Gov. Cox signed the bill March 26, 2026.

After the session: on March 12, 2026, The Salt Lake Tribune reported that Abbott said one of his bills was killed, and that he felt mocked, after he questioned the trail money. The Tribune described an end-of-session video image showing him behind the budget chair with the caption "You prob-ly should vote for Val's budget or forfeit your RFA." An RFA is a request for appropriation.

Reading note: only the opening paragraphs of the Tribune story were available (the rest is behind a paywall). Which bill Abbott was referring to, and the rest of his account, have not been read. These are Abbott's statements as reported, not findings of this case file.

Also in the record: Rep. Lisonbee, the one House member recorded as not voting, sponsored H.A.R. 13, the $130 million "Prison Capacity Expansion" request. That amount is in this same bill (item 169); the Legislative Fiscal Analyst puts the final prison-capacity figure at $125.0 million after a second budget bill (H.B. 3). The roll call shows only that she did not vote, not why. Missing a vote is common.

Graded by the Collective Rubric

This is The Weber County Hive's standing five-part rubric, applied the same way across every piece: Power — does it add or remove a check on power the public would otherwise have no say over? Transparency — can the public actually see the process? Financial Accountability — who actually pays, and is that disclosed plainly? Environmental Impact — is the effect on water, wetlands, and wildlife treated as a real constraint or a box to check? Community Impact — who bears the cost or holds a stake, and did they get real standing to be heard? The grade is about these four grants, not about whether the trail is a good idea.

A C means the record meets what Utah law requires, the status quo. A B or A means the record goes beyond it. D means something is vague or bundled, or a public check is weakened; F means something required is missing, or a check is removed with nothing in its place. Grades can change: a single line showing the total, a reporting requirement, or published officers for the foundation would each move a grade up.

How This Could Have Been Clearer

  • One line item, or one intent statement, showing the full up-to-$10 million total and its four sources together
  • A report back to an appropriations committee written into the grant paragraphs, as the same bill does for other grants
  • New appropriation lines for the two grants that currently direct existing money, so readers can see what each fund gives up
  • The foundation's officers and directors listed on its state filing, matching who is named publicly, before it receives state money

Questions to Ask, and Who to Ask

  1. How much of the $10 million has actually been paid, and by which agency? Ask your own legislator, or the Legislative Fiscal Analyst's office, which tracks state spending.
  2. What did the Outdoor Adventure and Active Transportation accounts give up to fund this? Ask members of the Natural Resources and Transportation appropriations subcommittees.
  3. Why does the state business filing list no officers, when news reports name a chairman, a director and board members? Ask the foundation through its public website, pioneertrail.org.
  4. Was there a request for appropriation for the trail, and under what title? Ask Rep. Val Peterson or Sen. Jerry Stevenson, the budget chairs.
  5. How much more will be requested? The foundation told the Deseret News it plans to ask for millions more in 2027 and 2028. Watch the 2027 request list when it is posted on le.utah.gov, and ask your legislator how they will vote on it.
