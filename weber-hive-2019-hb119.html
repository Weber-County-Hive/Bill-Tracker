The Rewrite: Local Initiatives and Referendums After 2018

Months after voters passed three initiatives and Holladay voters blocked a development by referendum, the Legislature rewrote how local petitions work.

Posted Oct. 19, 2026 · Updated Oct. 19, 2026

Part of Ten Years of Petition Laws, 2016–2026: 35 changes to Utah's initiative and referendum rules, every one written and passed by the Legislature. H.B. 119 (2019) is one of them.

Short version

  • H.B. 119 (2019), primary sponsor Rep. Brad Daw, rewrote the rules for local initiatives and referendums. It passed in the session right after 2018, when voters approved Props 2, 3 and 4 and Holladay voters blocked a development by referendum (Lt. Governor, 2018 ballot initiative results, May 29, 2018; KSL, Nov. 28, 2018 (Holladay referendum upheld)).
  • It created a step where the city or county decides within 20 days whether a proposed referendum is "legally referable to voters," with a separate test for land use laws (H.B. 119 enrolled text).
  • It required larger cities and counties to draw voter participation areas, and petitions must reach the signature percentage in at least 75% of them (H.B. 119 enrolled text).
  • Land use referendums need 15% to 40% of active voters, compared with 7.5% to 35% for other local laws (H.B. 119 enrolled text).
  • Daw said, "There was no attempt or no intent to tilt the scales one way or the other" (KPCW, Feb. 5, 2019).

What the bill says

Quoted from the enrolled bill (H.B. 119 enrolled text). Bill line numbers shown.

Utah Code 20A-7-602.8 (land use referability), lines 1516–1529

Within 20 days after the day on which an eligible voter files an application to circulate a referendum petition ... the county, city, town, or metro township to which the referendum pertains shall: (a) review the application to determine whether the proposed referendum is legally referable to voters

For land use laws it adds a ground: the referendum "challenges a land use decision, rather than a land use regulation" (lines 1528–1529). Sponsors have seven days to appeal; for other local laws, 10 (H.B. 119 enrolled text).

Utah Code 20A-7-401.3, voter participation areas, lines 288–306

divide the metro township, city, or county into eight contiguous and compact voter participation areas of substantially equal population

Required of first- and second-class cities and counties and large metro townships; third- and fourth-class places get four areas (H.B. 119 enrolled text).

Utah Code 20A-7-405, public meetings, lines 631–647

A county or municipality may not discuss a proposed initiative, an initiative, a proposed referendum, or a referendum at a public meeting unless

the meeting gives "equal time, within a reasonable limit, for presentations on both sides" and is held at the normal time or "at or after 6 p.m." (H.B. 119 enrolled text)

The timeline

  1. May 29, 2018 The Lt. Governor declares Props 2, 3 and 4 sufficient for the ballot (Lt. Governor, 2018 ballot initiative results, May 29, 2018).
  2. Nov. 6, 2018 Voters pass Props 2, 3 and 4. Holladay voters reject the old Cottonwood Mall development by referendum, "with 58 percent against" (KSL, Nov. 28, 2018 (Holladay referendum upheld)).
  3. Nov. 28–30, 2018 The Utah Supreme Court upholds the Holladay referendum. The Salt Lake Chamber says it will seek referendum changes in 2019 (KSL, Nov. 28, 2018 (Holladay referendum upheld); Salt Lake Tribune, Nov. 30, 2018).
  4. Jan. 22, 2019 H.B. 119 introduced (H.B. 119 (2019) bill status page).
  5. March 5, 2019 House passes it 64–7–4 (House 3rd reading roll call, March 5, 2019).
  6. March 14, 2019 Senate passes it 21–1–7; House concurs 59–12–4 (Senate roll call, March 14, 2019; House concurrence roll call, March 14, 2019).
  7. March 25, 2019 Gov. Gary Herbert signs it; effective May 14, 2019 (H.B. 119 (2019) bill status page).

What happened next

  • Pleasant Grove, 2024: the city attorney rejected a referendum on a budget amendment as not referable, under the non-land-use referability section H.B. 119 created (Daily Herald, Feb. 2, 2024 (Pleasant Grove)).
  • Box Elder County, May 28, 2026: the county attorney rejected referendum applications on two Stratos resolutions as not legally referable (Box Elder County release, May 28, 2026).
  • The thresholds and referability sections have been amended since 2019, including by H.B. 462 (2022) and S.B. 199 (2023).

Where it fits

How this law builds on the ones before it, from How they stack.

Local land-use referendums

  1. 2016 H.B. 10 B−
    widened "local law" so residents can refer more kinds of local action
  2. 2019 H.B. 119 D
    added a step to decide whether a measure is "legally referable to voters," and land-use rules
  3. 2021 H.B. 23 C+
    changed the definition of a land-use law and same-year ballot timing
  4. 2022 H.B. 462 D
    some transit-area land-use laws not referable after a two-thirds council vote; higher signature counts for the rest
  5. 2023 S.B. 199 D
    introduced as two-thirds for any land-use law; passed as "municipal" land-use laws not referable after a unanimous vote
  6. 2024 S.B. 100 C+
    opened some revenue-bond decisions to local referendum

Where it ended up: It started wider in 2016. Since 2019, three of these laws (H.B. 119, H.B. 462 and S.B. 199) set conditions that keep some land-use laws off the ballot. S.B. 100 added a kind of decision that is not a land-use law.
