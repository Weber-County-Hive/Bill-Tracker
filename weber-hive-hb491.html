CASE 2023-491-BSW

H.B. 491 · 2023 General Session · Bait-and-Switch

The Great Salt Lake Bill and Its Own Sponsor's Water Applications

A bill creating the Great Salt Lake Commissioner position also added a new records exemption for the Commissioner's water-related records, and cut the bill's own appropriation by 75% — changes made in the Senate substitute, not the version the bill's sponsor presented in the House.

Bill Sponsor: Rep. Mike SchultzFloor/Substitute Sponsor: Sen. Scott D. Sandall Effective: July 1, 2023 Became Law

New Here? Start Simple

H.B. 491 created the Great Salt Lake Commissioner position and expanded the Board of Water Resources to include a lake representative.

Between the House and Senate substitutes, two things changed with no explanation recorded in the committee minutes: a $40 million appropriation to the Great Salt Lake Account was cut to $10 million, and a new records exemption (63G-2-305(86)) was added shielding the Commissioner's water-related communications from public records requests.

The records exemption took effect July 1, 2023. Fifty-six days later, Rep. Schultz's own company, Keller Cattle Corp, filed two water-right applications (13-4099 and 13-4100) near Promontory. The State Engineer approved both on July 18, 2024.

Graded against the standing rubric

This isn't a personal opinion of the bill. The grade below asks two questions, applied the same way to every bill in this series — not whether any one person likes the outcome: 1. Power — does it add or remove a check on power that the public, collectively, would otherwise have no say over? 2. Transparency — does it add or remove what the public can actually see about the process, regardless of who ends up holding the final decision? Design and outcome are graded separately because a bill can be structurally sound and still fail to become law, or pass in a form very different from how it started — collapsing the two into one score would hide which one actually happened.

Graded by the Collective Rubric

This is The Weber County Hive's standing five-part rubric, applied the same way across every piece: Power — does it add or remove a check on power the public would otherwise have no say over? Transparency — can the public actually see the process? Financial Accountability — who actually pays, and is that disclosed plainly? Environmental Impact — is the effect on water, wetlands, and wildlife treated as a real constraint or a box to check? Community Impact — who bears the cost or holds a stake in this decision, and did they get real standing to be heard?

How This Could Have Actually Protected the Public

  • Debate a funding cut of this size (75%) on the record, in the chamber where the bill's sponsor doesn't sit
  • Require any new GRAMA exemption tied to a bill's sponsor or their immediate family/business interests to get a standalone, disclosed vote
  • Publish a plain-language notice whenever a new records exemption is about to take effect, so the public knows what becomes harder to review and when
