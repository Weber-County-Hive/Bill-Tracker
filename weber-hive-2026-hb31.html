H.B. 31 (2026): School Spending Reports and Contractor Pay

Utah posts what schools spend on Transparent Utah. This 2026 bill would have kept pay that a private contractor gives its own workers off that list. It died. Here is what it said, what was going on at the same time, and how it grades.

Short version

  • Every Utah school district and charter school reports its spending to the state’s public website, Transparent Utah, so anyone can look it up.
  • H.B. 31, by Rep. R. Neil Walter of Santa Clara, would have set common job names for reports and made schools report employee benefits. Its rewritten version (Substitute 1) would also have said that pay a private contractor gives its own workers is not “public financial information” (Substitute 1 text).
  • At the same time, the State Auditor, Tina Cannon, went to court on Feb. 10, 2026, to get pay records from American Preparatory Schools, the private company that runs American Preparatory Academy. The company was paid about $31 million in public money since 2022. Its executive director, named in the contract, is Carolyn Sharette (Salt Lake Tribune, Feb. 23, 2026).
  • The company first opposed the auditor’s request. It later said it “intends to voluntarily comply” (Salt Lake Tribune).
  • Walter said the bill grew out of an audit of Alpine School District and was meant to make reports match. He said it was not tied to the charter school dispute (St. George News, Feb. 28, 2026).
  • The bill never got a committee vote. It died March 6, 2026.
  • Graded by the collective rubric: D+. Reporting benefits and using common job names would help. Taking contractor pay out of public records would hide where some public school money goes.

The bill

H.B. 31 (2026): Local Education Agency Financial Reporting DIED

Primary sponsor: Rep. R. Neil Walter · No Senate sponsor listed · Died March 6, 2026 (enacting clause struck, filed) · Official bill page

In plain words: This would have made school spending reports use the same job names and show benefits. But it would also have kept the pay a private contractor gives its own workers off the public list. It never got a vote.

What it does (from the bill’s own words):

  • Requires common naming for jobs in school spending reports (original bill).
  • Clarifies what counts as “public financial information” for a school district or charter school (original bill).
  • Substitute 1: leaves out “payments a third-party contractor or vendor makes to employees of the third-party contractor or vendor.”
  • Substitute 1: requires schools to report employee benefits, and lets the State Auditor make rules about what contractors must disclose.

What was going on at the same time

The auditor’s court requestOn Feb. 10, 2026, State Auditor Tina Cannon asked a 3rd District Court judge to hold American Preparatory Schools in contempt for not turning over payroll, pay and spending records for public money paid to it since 2022. The company had given only yearly lump-sum totals (Salt Lake Tribune).
The company’s sideAmerican Preparatory Academy said its top administrators are paid by a separate private management company, and the school does not hold that payroll data (St. George News). The company later said it intends to comply voluntarily (Salt Lake Tribune).
The sponsor’s sideRep. Walter said the bill came from a legislative audit of Alpine School District that found job titles and pay were reported in different ways. He said it was not directly tied to the charter school dispute, and that it would not move forward (St. George News).
Where Carolyn Sharette fitsSharette is the executive director of American Preparatory Schools, named in its contract, and a founder of American Preparatory Academy (Salt Lake Tribune). Her ties to Hope of Utah and the book-list groups are traced on the Education page Who Connects to Whom.

What would make this clearer for the public

These come from gaps in the record, not from either side of the debate.

1. Post contractor pay for public schoolsWhen a private company runs a public school, posting what it pays its leaders out of public money would answer the question the auditor asked. Who could do it: the Legislature or the State Auditor.
2. Keep the good partsCommon job names and benefit reporting would make every district’s numbers easier to compare. Who could do it: any lawmaker, in a new bill.
3. Post the court outcomeA public update on the auditor’s case would show whether the records were turned over. Who could do it: the State Auditor.
