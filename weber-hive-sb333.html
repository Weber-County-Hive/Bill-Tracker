A Stadium-Shaped Loophole

MIDA's own Vice Chair wrote a new law letting any city or county carve out a 40-year tax-capture zone around a sporting venue — using the exact same toolkit this Hive has already documented for MIDA and the Inland Port Authority.

Companion piece: The One Authority to Rule Them All — S.B. 337, the related 2025 bill that would have created a seventh authority in this same family. Died without a vote.

Quick glossary — click a term
Major sporting event venue zone
The new zone type S.B. 333 creates — a defined area around a major sporting venue where a city or county can capture property and sales tax growth for its own use, for 25 to 40 years, subject to state committee approval.
Fiscal agent
The entity that actually administers a zone's captured funds — the bill allows either a community reinvestment agency or a Public Infrastructure District (PID) to fill this role.
Tax increment
The growth in tax revenue above a frozen starting point ("base year"). A zone captures that growth for itself; the underlying taxing entities (schools, county, city) keep only what the base year already generated.

Utah already has MIDA, the Inland Port Authority, and a growing list of tax-increment tools built for specific purposes. S.B. 333 adds a new one, purpose-built for sporting venues, sponsored by the senator who sits at the top of MIDA's own board. It uses the identical toolkit — tax increment capture, a PID as fiscal agent, decades-long terms, state-committee sign-off — this Hive has already documented for MIDA and UIPA. Worth noting up front: this is a separate, general statewide law. It is not the mechanism behind Salt Lake City's Delta Center renovation, which runs on a different law, the Capital City Revitalization Zone Act (2024).

What The Bill Does

+

S.B. 333, "Major Sporting Event Venue Financing Amendments," 2025 General Session. Chief Sponsor Sen. Jerry W. Stevenson; House Sponsor Rep. Jon Hawkins. Enacts a new chapter of state law, the Major Sporting Event Venue Zone Act (Utah Code Title 63N, Chapter 3, Part 17). Tap a card below to see what each piece actually does.

The Sponsor Sits On MIDA's Own Board

+

Sen. Jerry W. Stevenson (SD-6) is MIDA's sitting Vice Chair, one of the authority's three longest-serving current members — on the board since 2008, one year after MIDA was created. This Hive has already documented his MIDA seat, and his history sponsoring MIDA's own power-expansion bills, at length in a separate profile.

S.B. 333 doesn't touch MIDA's own statute (Title 63H) — it's a standalone law, not a MIDA Act amendment. But the underlying toolkit is the same one MIDA already runs on: a defined capture zone, decades-long tax-increment terms, a state committee sign-off process, and a PID available as fiscal agent. The senator who helped build and expand one authority working on that model wrote the law extending the same basic model to a new category of project, statewide.

The bill moved through five substitutes between introduction and passage. Notable changes along the way: an "accommodation tax" authority present in the original bill and the 3rd substitute was dropped from later versions. A provision requiring GOEO to "initiate an analysis of the feasibility" of a proposed zone, present in the 3rd and 4th substitutes, was also removed by the final version — leaving the review committee's own evaluation as the primary check, without a separate mandated feasibility study.

Still Open

+

Correction (8/26/26): Summit County's own government website explicitly names S.B. 333 as the authorizing statute for a proposed "Impacted Communities Tax" — using the bill's provision letting a third-class county with three or more major sporting event venues impose up to a 1.1% sales tax in its unincorporated areas. The Snyderville Basin/Park City area qualifies on venue count (Deer Valley, Utah Olympic Park, and others). Estimated revenue: roughly $17 million a year at the full 1.1% rate. Restricted by statute to "public infrastructure and infrastructure improvements, including transportation and transit projects" — the county's stated targets are Kimball Junction/I-80 interchange work, a transit center and bus rapid transit along SR-224, structured parking with workforce housing, and a pedestrian bridge over SR-224, explicitly framed around 2034 Olympics preparation. A County Council work session was held October 22, 2025, with a vote on the ordinance scheduled October 29, 2025 — imposed by ordinance alone, no public vote required.

What isn't confirmed yet: whether Summit County's Council actually adopted the ordinance on October 29, 2025 or since — the county's public meeting-minutes archive for that date wasn't locatable through search. The bill's exact floor vote tallies and governor's signature date also haven't been pulled from the official record, though ABC4 (Dec 30, 2025) confirms S.B. 333 took effect January 1, 2026, among that year's batch of new Utah laws. The review committee's exact membership (referenced in the bill but not detailed here) hasn't been pulled either. This is a live thread worth a direct follow-up with Summit County (Finance Officer Matt Leavitt, mleavitt@summitcountyutah.gov) rather than a closed question.
