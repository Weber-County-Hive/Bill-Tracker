CASE 2026-044 · 2026 GENERAL SESSION

S.B. 44 — Statewide Resource Management Plan Amendments

Status: Became law · Signed March 26, 2026

Became Law
Mixed · Leaning Concerning
"It creates statewide coordination but adopts major public-land policy through a two-page bill that does not show readers what changed inside the 198-page plan."
What It Did

Swapped in an entirely new 198-page plan — by reference

S.B. 44 replaced Utah's 2025 State Resource Management Plan with a new plan dated May 6, 2026. The bill itself is short: it adopts the new plan, supersedes the old one, requires the Public Lands Policy Coordinating Office to monitor federal, state, and local compliance as funding allows, requires that office to report recommended changes and implementation information to the Federalism Commission annually, gives the Commission authority to recommend future modifications, and requires legislative approval before any modification takes effect.

The bill contains no description of the individual policies being adopted — it simply refers readers to the separate plan "on file with the office."

Existing Utah law requires state agencies and local governments to refer to and "substantially conform" with the adopted statewide plan when making plans involving public lands or other public resources — which is what makes the plan's content, not just the bill's text, the thing that actually matters.

Why The Separate Plan Matters

198 pages covering nearly everything

The adopted plan isn't an informational brochure — it's a binding reference document agencies and political subdivisions must substantially conform with. It covers:

AgricultureAir QualityEnergy Development Fire & Forest ManagementGrazingMining & Minerals Outdoor RecreationPipelines & Utility Corridors Water Rights & QualityWildlife Threatened & Endangered SpeciesPublic-Land Access

The Public Lands Policy Coordinating Office must also identify local or agency plans that are inconsistent with the statewide plan and recommend how those inconsistencies should be addressed — meaning this plan doesn't just describe state priorities, it can be used to flag and pressure local plans that don't match.

Flagged: The Mill Creek Canyon Provision

A state-park push, buried inside the plan

One significant addition appears in the outdoor-recreation section. The plan says Mill Creek Canyon — currently managed by the U.S. Forest Service and Salt Lake County — could benefit from more consistent services, infrastructure, and a unified long-term vision.

"Utah State Parks proposes to elevate the visitor experience" through amenities, trails, year-round maintenance, and expanded programming.

The plan goes on to describe Mill Creek Canyon as potentially becoming one of the most accessible parks in the state system. That language doesn't itself transfer ownership or formally create a state park — but it establishes state management of the canyon as an approved statewide policy objective.

Save Our Canyons opposed the provision, arguing it could shift a locally supported Forest Service–county management arrangement toward state control, and noting the canyon is already receiving improvements through a $15 million federal land-access grant. (Save Our Canyons statement)

What It Did Not Do

The limits of the bill

  • ✕ Transfer federal land to Utah
  • ✕ Formally create a new state park
  • ✕ Appropriate money for Mill Creek Canyon
  • ✕ Authorize construction of specific facilities
  • ✕ Eliminate federal environmental review
  • ✕ Give Utah legal ownership of federally managed land

Any actual transfer, lease, cooperative-management agreement, or major development would require additional legal and administrative steps beyond this bill.

Who It Helped / Who Could Be Harmed

Depends entirely on how agencies use the plan

Who It Helped

  • The Public Lands Policy Coordinating Office, which gets a unified statewide policy framework
  • The Federalism Commission, which reviews changes and implementation
  • State officials seeking more influence over federally managed public lands
  • Counties whose resource priorities are reflected in the statewide plan
  • Recreation and tourism interests supporting additional facilities and state investment
  • Agriculture, grazing, energy, mining, and infrastructure interests addressed throughout the plan

Who Could Be Harmed

  • Local governments whose plans differ from the statewide policy
  • Residents who prefer existing local and federal management arrangements
  • Conservation interests concerned about expanded development or extraction
  • Wildlife and habitat, if economic-development objectives outweigh conservation
  • Taxpayers, if plan objectives later produce new state parks, infrastructure, litigation, or land-management obligations
  • The public, who can't easily determine what changed between annual versions of a lengthy external plan
Who Paid

No appropriation — but that only covers adoption, not implementation

Fiscal Finding
No measurable state expenditure
No measurable local-government cost
No direct tax or fee expense for residents or businesses
No immediate regulatory-burden change

That finding applies only to adopting the plan itself — it does not estimate the future cost of implementing individual projects or management proposals contained within it, including Mill Creek Canyon.

The Public-Interest Bottom Line

A statewide resource plan can genuinely help agencies coordinate land, water, recreation, and infrastructure policy — that's a legitimate public benefit. The accountability concern is how Utah adopted it: lawmakers unanimously approved a two-page bill that incorporated a separate 198-page policy document, without the bill text summarizing the plan's substantive revisions, identifying additions like the Mill Creek Canyon proposal, or giving readers a side-by-side comparison to the prior version.

Should major land-management policies become state law through an external document when the adopting bill doesn't clearly disclose what was added, removed, or changed?
