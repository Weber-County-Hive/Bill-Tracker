S.B. 312 (2026): Educator License Amendments

When the state takes away or pauses a teacher’s license, can that person still work or volunteer at a school? This 2026 law says it depends on what they did. Here is what it does, who voted how, and how it grades.

Short version

  • In Utah, the State School Board can suspend (pause) or revoke (take away) a teacher’s license. A group called UPPAC looks into the complaint first and tells the board what it found.
  • Before this law, a person who could not get a license for any of the reasons in the law could not work or volunteer in any public school (bill text, struck words in Section 53E-6-603).
  • S.B. 312 splits cases into two kinds. For serious misconduct (harm or a real risk of harm to a child, sexual misconduct, grooming, abuse or violence), the person still cannot work or volunteer at a public school. For other cases, like paperwork mistakes, the ban applies only if the board writes down a specific risk to students.
  • Primary sponsor: Sen. John D. Johnson of North Ogden. House sponsor: Rep. Doug Welton. The House version (Substitute 1) lists Rep. Karianne Lisonbee as its sponsor.
  • It passed the Senate 16–11 and the House 45–22. The Senate then agreed to the House changes by one vote, 15–14. Gov. Spencer Cox signed it March 26, 2026. It took effect May 6, 2026.
  • Jamie Renda, founder of Path Forward Utah, supported it and called it the first of several changes she wants to UPPAC (KSL/Deseret News, March 25, 2026). Renda signed the state’s grant contract for Hope of Utah as its “Co-Founder.” The Utah Education Association and State School Board member Carol Lear opposed it.
  • Graded by the collective rubric: C. It adds written findings and better notice for teachers. It also narrows a rule that used to keep people out of schools automatically.

The bill

S.B. 312 (2026): Educator License Amendments LAW

Primary sponsor: Sen. John D. Johnson · House sponsor: Rep. Doug Welton · House version (Substitute 1) sponsor: Rep. Karianne Lisonbee · Signed March 26, 2026 · In effect May 6, 2026 · Official bill page

In plain words: If a teacher hurt a child or put a child at real risk, they still can’t work or volunteer at a school. If the problem was a mistake that put no child at risk, the board has to write down why the person is a danger before it can keep them out.

What it does (from the bill’s own words):

  • Sets up two kinds of cases after a license is suspended or revoked: “qualifying serious misconduct” and everything else.
  • Serious misconduct means harm or a credible risk of harm to a child, sexual misconduct, grooming, boundary violations, abuse, exploitation, violence, serious endangerment, or an ongoing threat to student safety. For these, the work-and-volunteer ban is automatic.
  • For other cases, the ban applies only if the board issues written findings of “a specific, articulable risk” and why a smaller step is not enough.
  • Says “technical violations” (paperwork, record-keeping or communication mistakes, or honest misstatements not about student safety) cannot lead to a ban unless there is a pattern and written findings.
  • Lets schools set stricter rules of their own.
  • Requires the notice before a hearing to explain these work and volunteer limits.
  • Applies to cases opened on or after May 6, 2026.

Who spoke for and against

For itAt the House Education Committee on March 4, 2026: Sandra Buendia, Jennie Jonsson, Maryann Christensen (Utah Legislative Watch) and Pam Pedersen (committee minutes). In the news: Jamie Renda of Path Forward Utah (KSL/Deseret News). Renda is not listed as a speaker in the House committee minutes.
Against itAt the same hearing: Sara Jones (Utah Education Association), Carol Lear (State School Board member), Rebecca Kirkman (teacher) and Matt Hymas (State School Board chair). The UEA said the bill could weaken consequences for educators (UEA statement).
What the sponsor saidSen. Johnson said the bill does not reduce UPPAC’s authority. He described a veteran educator, suspended for two years, who could not volunteer at her grandson’s school (KSL/Deseret News).
What came nextThe Legislature’s 2026 Education Interim Committee put UPPAC complaint handling and teacher due process on its study list and heard it May 20, 2026 (study list).

What would make this clearer for the public

These come from gaps in the record, not from either side of the debate.

1. Count the casesPost how many suspensions and revocations fall into each kind (serious or not), and how many came with written findings. Who could do it: the State School Board.
2. Post the findings, without namesA public summary of each written finding, with names removed, would show how the new test is used. Who could do it: the State School Board.
