Ten Years of Petition Laws, 2016–2026

How Utah's rules for citizen initiatives and referendums changed, law by law.

Posted Oct. 19, 2026 · Updated Oct. 19, 2026

The rules didn't change themselves. Every one of these 35 changes was written and passed by the Legislature: 33 bills and 2 constitutional amendments it put on the ballot. None of these petition rules came from a citizen vote.
For the story of how these laws changed, read The Rules Keep Changing on the Referendum docket. This page is the law-by-law reference table.

Short version

  • From 2016 to 2026 we found 35 laws that changed how Utahns use initiatives and referendums. 30 took effect (some only for a time), 2 were repeals that ended a pending referendum, 1 never took effect, 1 (Amendment D) was voided by the courts, and 1 (Amendment B) is on the Nov. 3, 2026 ballot.
  • By what they did: 10 added requirements, 3 removed requirements, 20 were mixed, and the 2 repeals changed no process rule. 7 apply to local measures, 11 to statewide measures and 17 to both.
  • The 30 laws that took effect average 1.98 (C) on the Hive's rubric. None got an A or an F. The lowest grade among them is D. Two repeals that ended referendums, H.B. 185 (2020) and H.B. 2001 (2025), got an F.
  • 2016 and 2017 were the highest years (C+). 2023 was the lowest year among those with laws in effect (1.65, C−).
  • H.B. 32 (2026) requires online training for anyone gathering signatures, rejects packets from untrained gatherers, and starting July 1, 2027 tells signers whether their signature counted. Grade: C+.

How grades work. A C means the record meets what Utah law requires, the status quo. A B or A means it went beyond it, and the record shows it. D means something is vague or bundled, or a public check is weakened; F means something required is missing, or a check is removed with nothing in its place.

Overall = the average of Power, Transparency, Financial accountability and Community impact (A=4, B=3, C=2, D=1, F=0; Environmental is N/A and left out); letters sit on whole numbers, so 2.75 is B−, not B+.

Four grades on this page are copied as published in The Rules Keep Changing (Referendum docket): H.B. 119 (2019), H.B. 218 (2022), H.B. 38 (2023) and S.B. 199 (2023). H.B. 185 (2020) is graded as published in the Hive's Bill-Tracker case file on S.B. 2001 and H.B. 185, and H.B. 2001 (2025), which did the same thing, gets the same grades. Published grades stand. For the averages, those published letters are counted as D = 1.0, C = 2.0, C− = 1.75 and F = 0, so the in-effect average here is 1.98 (C); all 35 together average 1.84 (C).

On Oct. 19, 2026, four grades were tightened after a check against the rubric's own words: H.B. 462 (Transparency C → D), H.B. 75 (Power C → F), H.B. 242 (Transparency B → D) and S.B. 43 (Financial C → D). The reason is in each law's grade details.

Grades are the publisher's rubric applied to each bill's own summary and text. They are not legal conclusions.

When it widened, when it tightened

This lines up what citizens and courts did with what lawmakers passed next. We say "stated" only when a sponsor, legislative leaders or the bill's own effect ties a law to a citizen measure, and "timing only" when just the dates line up. We do not guess at anyone's reasons.

When it widened. The laws graded "helps": H.B. 10 (2016), S.B. 69 (2017), H.B. 79 (2024), and S.B. 4003 (2024), which never took effect. Two more opened access without reaching a B: S.B. 107 (2024) dropped the gatherer residency rule, and S.B. 100 (2024) opened some revenue-bond decisions to referendum.

When it tightened. All six "hinders" grades are from 2019 or later. Four came within weeks or months of citizens winning something: H.B. 119 (the session after the 2018 votes), H.B. 185 (the day a referendum qualified), Amendment D (six weeks after the Prop 4 ruling) and H.B. 2001 (after a referendum qualified). For S.B. 199, the sponsor gave a general reason: that the referendum process was "being weaponized to hurt developers and cities." For H.B. 462 we found only timing, no stated link.

2018

Six initiatives gathering signatures, then three pass

Citizens and courts

While six initiatives were gathering signatures, the Utah Patients Coalition submitted medical cannabis signatures on April 16, 2018 (ULCT Board minutes, April 25, 2018 (PDF page 5)). On May 29, 2018, the Lieutenant Governor declared Props 2, 3 and 4 "Sufficient" for the ballot; the Direct Primary Election initiative was declared "Insufficient" (Lt. Governor, 2018 ballot initiative results, May 29, 2018). Count My Vote stayed off the ballot after the Keep My Voice signature-removal campaign (KUER, Aug. 24, 2018). On Nov. 6, voters passed Props 2, 3 and 4, and Holladay voters rejected the old Cottonwood Mall development by referendum, "with 58 percent against" (KSL, Nov. 28, 2018).

The food-tax referendum

Citizens and courts

2018–2023

Local land-use referendums

Citizens and courts

Holladay, 2018 (KSL, Nov. 28, 2018); Coalville, where a rezoning referendum qualified in February 2020 (KSL, Feb. 2020). In 2023, Mountain Green residents filed a referendum on a Morgan County rezone; the newspaper tied the filing to S.B. 199 and reported the commission vote was 4–1 (Morgan County News, May 15, 2023). S.B. 199's unanimous-vote rule, as passed, applies to a "municipal land use law." The code section it points to for that term (20A-7-101) does not define it, so whether the rule reaches county votes is unsettled (Justia, 2023 and 2025 copies of 20A-7-101).

→

What lawmakers did next

2022: H.B. 462 made transit-area land-use laws passed by a two-thirds council vote not referable, and raised the signatures needed for the others. 2023: S.B. 199, as introduced, would have done the same for any land-use law passed by a two-thirds vote; a House floor amendment on March 1 narrowed it to a "municipal land use law" passed "by a unanimous vote of the local legislative body."

H.B. 462 D S.B. 199 D

Is there a link?

Stated, as a general reason, for S.B. 199. Sen. Mike McKell, its sponsor: "What's happening across the state is the referendum process is being weaponized to hurt developers and cities." He did not name a referendum; the Deseret News story tied the bill to a Spanish Fork referendum on a 668-unit development, which fell short of signatures in 2022, and Spanish Fork officials supported the bill (Deseret News, Feb. 14, 2023). Timing only, for H.B. 462. We found no sponsor statement or news story tying it to a particular referendum.

2024

The Prop 4 ruling and Amendment D

Citizens and courts

July 11, 2024: the Utah Supreme Court ruled that the Legislature's power to change certain initiatives has limits (Utah News Dispatch, Oct. 24, 2024).

→

What lawmakers did next

Aug. 21, 2024, six weeks later: the Legislature put Amendment D (S.J.R. 401) on the ballot (S.J.R. 401 (2024) bill page). A judge voided it Sept. 12, 2024, and the Utah Supreme Court upheld that (Utah News Dispatch, Oct. 24, 2024), for two reasons: the required newspaper notice was not published, and the ballot language was misleading (Hive, The Amendments on Your Ballot, Part 1). The next year, the Legislature passed Amendment A (H.J.R. 10, 2025), which would remove that newspaper rule; it is on the Nov. 3, 2026 ballot.

How they stack

Most of these laws build on earlier ones. Each chain below follows one part of the process in order, with each law's grade and label.

Local land-use referendums

  1. 2016 H.B. 10 B− Helps
    widened "local law" so residents can refer more kinds of local action
  2. 2019 H.B. 119 D Hinders
    added a step to decide whether a measure is "legally referable to voters," and land-use rules
  3. 2021 H.B. 23 C+ Neutral
    changed the definition of a land-use law and same-year ballot timing
  4. 2022 H.B. 462 D Hinders
    some transit-area land-use laws not referable after a two-thirds council vote; higher signature counts for the rest
  5. 2023 S.B. 199 D Hinders
    introduced as two-thirds for any land-use law; passed as "municipal" land-use laws not referable after a unanimous vote
  6. 2024 S.B. 100 C+ Neutral
    opened some revenue-bond decisions to local referendum

Where it ended up: It started wider in 2016. Since 2019, three of these laws (H.B. 119, H.B. 462 and S.B. 199) set conditions that keep some land-use laws off the ballot. S.B. 100 added a kind of decision that is not a land-use law.

Repeal ends the referendum

  1. 2020 H.B. 185 F Hinders
    repealed the food-tax law the day its referendum qualified (about 152,000 signatures)
  2. 2020 H.B. 75 C− Neutral
    wrote it into law: a referendum petition "is void if the Legislature repeals the proposed law"; also added a temporary stay
  3. 2025 H.B. 2001 F Hinders
    repealed the union law; the referendum with more than 320,000 signatures ended

Where it ended up: Twice, referendums that had qualified ended without a vote. Each repeal got an F. The rule in between, H.B. 75, gets a Power F for the same reason; it lands at C− overall because it also added a temporary stay.

Signature gatherers

  1. 2019 H.B. 145 C Neutral
    new crimes, including paying for a referendum signature or its removal
  2. 2021 H.B. 136 C− Neutral
    gatherer rules with penalties; no per-signature pay
  3. 2024 S.B. 107 C+ Neutral
    dropped the in-state residency rule for gatherers
  4. 2026 H.B. 32 C+ Neutral
    online training before gathering; packets from untrained gatherers rejected; kept hourly-only pay

Where it ended up: Each round added rules for gatherers except 2024, which removed one.

Taking your signature back

  1. 2019 H.B. 145 C Neutral
    changed the form for removing a signature
  2. 2021 H.B. 211 C Neutral
    made state and local forms and steps the same, including removals
  3. 2023 H.B. 68 C Neutral
    put the rules for gathering and removing signatures in one place
  4. 2024 H.B. 79 B− Helps
    added a way for people with a disability to sign or remove a signature
  5. 2026 H.B. 242 C+ Neutral
    rules for people who gather removals: badges, a notice, hourly pay, no prepaid postage

Where it ended up: Removal rules grew from one form in 2019 to their own set of gatherer rules in 2026.

Tax and money initiatives

  1. 2017 H.B. 255 C+ Neutral
    the percentage of a proposed tax increase must be shown on the petition and in voter materials
  2. 2019 S.B. 151 C+ Neutral
    applications must state "funding sources for the proposed law"
  3. 2020 S.B. 143 C Neutral
    changed the fiscal impact statement
  4. 2025 S.B. 73 C+ Neutral
    the application and fiscal statement must say how a proposed law is funded
  5. 2025 S.J.R. 2 (Amendment B) C− Neutral
    would require 60% to pass a tax initiative; on the Nov. 3, 2026 ballot

Where it ended up: The first four changed what voters are told about cost. Amendment B would change the vote needed to pass.

The Legislature and voter-passed laws

  1. 2019 H.B. 133 C− Neutral
    changed when a law passed by statewide initiative takes effect
  2. 2019 H.B. 195 C Neutral
    thresholds based on active voters; no "substantially similar" initiative within two years
  3. 2024 S.J.R. 401 (Amendment D) D− Hinders
    the Legislature's power to amend or repeal voter-passed laws; voided by the courts
  4. 2024 S.B. 4003 B− Helps
    would have extended the referendum deadline from 40 to 60 days; tied to Amendment D, never took effect

Where it ended up: The biggest change in this chain, Amendment D, was voided, and the one piece that would have given citizens more time went with it.

What voters are told

  1. 2016 H.B. 198 C+ Neutral
    rules for arguments for and against ballot measures
  2. 2017 S.B. 69 B− Helps
    voters must be told about local ballot measures
  3. 2022 S.B. 38 C+ Neutral
    a short ballot title and summary replaced the long title
  4. 2023 H.B. 69 C Neutral
    dropped the separate ballot measure insert when the same information is on the ballot
  5. 2023 S.B. 43 C− Neutral
    pre-circulation hearing notices for initiatives moved from the newspaper to class A notice
  6. 2025 Amendment A (H.J.R. 10) Not graded here
    on the Nov. 3, 2026 ballot: would remove the Constitution's rule that proposed amendments be printed in a newspaper in every county, and let the Legislature set the method by statute

Where it ended up: S.B. 43 moved initiative hearing notices out of the newspaper by statute in 2023. Amendment A would make the same kind of move for constitutional amendments, in the Constitution itself. In 2024, the missing newspaper notice was one of the two reasons the Utah Supreme Court gave for voiding Amendment D. Amendment A is not one of the 35 petition laws and is not graded here; see The Amendments on Your Ballot, Part 1.

Petition forgery allegations and signature safeguards

These cases are about who actually signed, who swore they gathered the signatures, and how gatherers were paid; none shows fraud in an election result. We have not verified each defendant's court outcome, and "not verified" does not mean a case is still pending. The state's 2026 Candidate Manual says more than 20 people have faced charges and pleaded guilty to felonies and misdemeanors tied to signature gathering in recent years, mostly for fraud and forgery, without naming them (2026 Candidate Manual, p. 18).

  • Notices to signers. From July 1, 2027, clerks tell signers whether their signature counted, which could help voters spot unauthorized use of their names. Email goes only if the voter file has an email address; texts need a mobile number and consent (H.B. 32 (2026) enrolled text).

A rule that helps catch forged signatures is different from a rule that raises how much support a petition needs. Forgery cases do not show that every limit on petitions prevents fraud, so we grade those questions separately.

H.B. 32 (2026) in full

Every version, vote and statement: the H.B. 32 case file.

H.B. 32 (2026), "Signature Gathering and Verification Amendments," changes how signatures are gathered and counted for every initiative and referendum in Utah, statewide and local. Rep. Paul A. Cutler was the primary sponsor and Sen. Ronald M. Winterton the floor sponsor. Our grade: C+ (Power D · Transparency B · Financial C · Environmental N/A · Community B).

Every requirement

  • Required training. The Lieutenant Governor's office builds an online course, and "An individual may not gather signatures for a petition unless the individual has, before collecting any signatures, completed the online training course" (20A-1-1005(4)).
  • A new form in every packet. Each packet carries a signature gatherer verification sheet, including "I completed the signature gatherer training available on the lieutenant governor's website." "A clerk shall reject a petition packet if: ... the individual who gathered signatures for the petition checked 'No'" or left the sheet incomplete (20A-1-1004(2)).
  • Badges. Paid signature gatherers must wear a badge on the front of their body while collecting.
  • Hourly pay only. No one may be paid to gather signatures "unless the payment is based on an hourly rate" (20A-7-104(1)). That rule already existed; the introduced bill and its first two substitutes would have allowed pay "per signature declared valid," and the final version kept hourly-only pay (bill versions on le.utah.gov).
  • Deadlines and markings. A clerk may not count a signature dated after the deadline, and sponsors may not mark or redact signature sheets.
  • Email field removed from signature sheets (source table).
  • A list of gatherers. For statewide initiatives, sponsors give the Lieutenant Governor the name and email address of every person who gathered signatures.
  • Packets kept 22 months. The bill's summary says it directs "the county clerk to retain and preserve each packet for at least 22 months." The operative text does this by cross-reference: the clerk "shall retain and preserve each verified packet in the manner described in Subsection 20A-4-202(2)" (20A-7-105(6)(b)(ii)), and that existing election-records law requires election returns to be kept "for at least 22 months after the date of the election" (20A-4-202(2)(c)). H.B. 32 does not change 20A-4-202 itself.

What it also adds for signers and sponsors

  • Notices to signers. Starting July 1, 2027, clerks email and text each signer whether their signature counted, "within two business days after the day on which the clerk declares a signer's signature valid or invalid" (20A-1-1002(3)). Email goes only if the voter file has the signer's email address; a text goes only if it has a mobile number and the signer consented on the voter registration form to official texts. The old law did not do this.
  • A "condensed" packet option. The bill's highlights call it "an alternative packet type." The definitions (20A-7-101) call it a "condensed" packet: a copy of the petition, the signature sheets, and the circulator verification sheet, bound together. Each signature sheet must "contain a printed QR code at the top of the signature sheet" linking to the Lieutenant Governor's website, next to the statement "This QR code provides access to a website that includes the full text of the law proposed by this initiative petition" (20A-7-203(3)(d)), with a plain-language summary. Sponsors can choose that or the traditional packet.
  • Verification statistics, one reason Transparency gets a B.
