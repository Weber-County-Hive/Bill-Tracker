CASE 2026-249

H.B. 249 · 2026 General Session

Federal Funds Modifications

Utah taxpayers are funding a public dashboard intended to show the state's dependence on federal money — but the bill sets no launch deadline and no real performance measures for whether it works.

Chief Sponsor: Rep. Calvin Roberts · Senate Sponsor: Sen. John Johnson Effective: FY2027 (dashboard funding begins) Became Law

New Here? Start Simple

"Mostly helpful — Utah taxpayers are funding a public dashboard intended to show the state’s dependence on federal money, although the bill does not establish a launch deadline or detailed performance measures."

The Analytics Solutions Center at Utah State University's Jon M. Huntsman School of Business must build and maintain a public digital dashboard showing Utah's dependence on federal funding, the risks of possible reductions, and economic scenarios to help the state prepare. The law does not itself reject federal funding or require any agency to end a federally supported program.

Making Utah's federal-funding dependence visible is a legitimate public benefit — federal money supports major portions of health care, transportation, education, nutrition, and public safety. A useful dashboard should show more than one large statewide total: the specific programs, the Utahns each one serves, the federal/state funding split, restrictions attached to the money, the consequences of a reduction, and whether Utah could realistically replace it. Whether this dashboard does that depends entirely on implementation the bill doesn't specify.

What Changed, Section by Section

ProvisionWhat it does
Federal-funding dependency dashboardMust be publicly available, user-friendly, dynamic, and kept accurate. The center must identify, clean, organize, and securely store the underlying data. Work is subject to legislative appropriations and done in consultation with the Federalism Commission.
Federalism Commission's expanded roleThe legislative fiscal analyst already runs periodic budget stress tests. H.B. 249 requires the federal-funds portions of those tests to go to the Federalism Commission, which reviews the results, decides on legislative recommendations, and submits them to the Executive Appropriations Committee.

What a good dashboard could reveal, if it's built well: how much federal money Utah receives; which agencies and programs depend most heavily on it; which services face the greatest risk if federal funds are reduced; how much replacement funding Utah would need; which losses the state could realistically absorb; which federal programs have no practical state-funded replacement.

The enrolled bill doesn't prescribe the exact categories, charts, program-level detail, or downloadable data that has to appear — so the answer to most of the above depends entirely on implementation.

What the law does not do: cut federal funding · reject a federal grant · require Utah to replace lost federal money · guarantee affected services would continue · establish a specific dashboard launch date · require program-by-program contingency plans · require an independent audit of the dashboard · set measures for evaluating whether the dashboard is worth its continuing cost.

Who It Helped

  • Residents seeking clearer information on where federal money goes
  • Lawmakers preparing for federal budget reductions or shutdowns
  • State agencies that need contingency-planning data
  • Researchers and journalists examining Utah's fiscal dependence
  • The Federalism Commission, which gains stress-test access and a recommendation role
  • USU's Analytics Solutions Center, which receives the funding to build it

Who Could Be Harmed

  • Taxpayers, if the dashboard duplicates existing federal or state financial data
  • Agencies, which must devote staff time to supplying and validating data
  • Residents, if it shows only agency-level totals with no program-level consequences
  • The debate itself, if federal funding gets framed as a federalism dispute rather than a service-delivery question
  • Vulnerable residents, if programs serving them are labeled "federal dependency" without explaining what losing them would cost

How This Could Have Actually Protected the Public

  • Set a specific dashboard launch date, rather than leaving the timeline entirely open-ended
  • Required program-level detail (which specific services, how many people, what the state/federal split is) rather than allowing agency-level totals alone
  • Required a periodic independent audit or public review of whether the dashboard is meeting its stated purpose
  • Set a measure for evaluating whether the dashboard is worth its continuing $150,000/year cost, rather than funding it indefinitely with no review trigger
