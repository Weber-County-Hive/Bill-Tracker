Ten Years of Petition Laws, in Plain Words

The short, simple version of our law-by-law reference page.

Plain words · Posted Oct. 19, 2026 · Updated Oct. 19, 2026

Want every law, vote and source? Read the full page

Utah voters have two ways to make or undo laws themselves. An initiative lets voters write a law and put it on the ballot. A referendum lets voters put a law that lawmakers already passed on the ballot, so voters can keep it or reject it. Both start with a petition and a lot of signatures.

The Legislature sets the rules for those petitions. From 2016 to 2026, we found 35 laws that changed them. Every one was written and passed by lawmakers. None of these petition rules came from a citizen vote.

What happened to them

30 took effect. Two were repeals that ended a referendum before voters got to decide: the food tax repeal in 2020 and the union law repeal in 2025. One never took effect. One, Amendment D, was voided by the courts before the votes were counted. One, Amendment B, is on your Nov. 3, 2026 ballot.

10 added new steps or limits. 3 removed some. 20 did some of both.

How we graded them

A C means a law meets what Utah law already requires. A B or A means it went further for the public. A D means a public check got weaker or the law is vague. An F means a check was removed with nothing in its place.

The laws that took effect average a C. None got an A or an F. Two repeals that ended referendums, in 2020 and 2025, got an F. Four grades come straight from our published series, The Rules Keep Changing.

What changed for you

  • Signing got easier in some ways. You can sign electronically with a gatherer (2022). People with disabilities have their own way to sign (2024). Gatherers no longer have to live in Utah (2024).
  • Some local decisions are off limits. Some land use laws can't be put to a vote if the council passed them by a big enough margin (2022 and 2023).
  • Lawmakers can end a referendum. If the Legislature repeals the law you are petitioning against, the petition is void (2020).
  • Gatherers now need training. Under H.B. 32 (2026), anyone gathering signatures must take an online state course first. If a gatherer marks "No," the clerk rejects the whole packet.
  • You'll find out if your signature counted. Starting July 1, 2027, your clerk must email or text you within two business days.
  • Tax initiatives may need 60%. If voters pass Amendment B on Nov. 3, citizen initiatives that raise taxes would need 60% of the vote.

Did each law help you, hurt you, or neither?

We sorted the laws by their grade. 4 helped the public (a B− or better). 25 were about neutral (a C). 6 hindered the public (a D or F).

When the rules opened up, and when they tightened

  • Opened up: mostly in 2016 and 2017, and again in 2024.
  • Tightened: all six of the "hindered" laws came in 2019 or later.
  • After voters won: four of those six passed within weeks or months of citizens winning something: after voters passed Props 2, 3 and 4 in 2018; the day the food tax referendum qualified in 2020; six weeks after the 2024 court ruling on Prop 4; and after the union referendum qualified in 2025.
  • The 2023 land use law: its sponsor said the referendum process was "being weaponized to hurt developers and cities."
  • Only timing: for the 2022 land use law, we found no lawmaker or news story tying it to a particular referendum.

We say a law is linked to a citizen measure only when a sponsor, legislative leaders or the law's own effect show it. We don't guess why anyone voted the way they did.

How the laws build on each other

  • Local land use votes: opened up in 2016, then narrowed in 2019, 2022 and 2023.
  • Repeal ends the vote: lawmakers repealed laws people were petitioning against in 2020 and 2025, and a 2020 law says that voids the petition.
  • Signature gatherers: more rules almost every round, ending with required training in 2026.
  • Tax initiatives: first more information about cost on the petition; now Amendment B would require 60% to pass.
  • What voters are told: notices moved from newspapers to other methods. In 2023 that happened for initiative hearings. Amendment A on this year's ballot would do the same for constitutional amendments. A missing newspaper notice was one reason the courts threw out Amendment D in 2024.

Who brought these changes

Most changes came from different lawmakers. A few names come up again and again. Rep. Jordan Teuscher carried 6 of the 35 laws, more than anyone else, and Sen. Curtis Bramble carried 5. Rep. Teuscher and Sen. Kirk Cullimore sponsored two of the six laws that hindered the public: Amendment D (2024) and the 2025 repeal that ended the union referendum. They also sponsored the union law itself.

Signature fraud

Three Utah cases since 2023 involved paid signature gatherers: two on candidate petitions, with people charged in 2024 and 2025, and one on the 2026 Prop 4 repeal, where the Utah County clerk sent packets to the county attorney. Charges were filed against individuals, not companies. In June 2026, one out-of-state gatherer in the Prop 4 repeal pleaded no contest to forgery in Uintah County. We have not verified the other individual court outcomes. In 2024 lawmakers dropped the rule that gatherers for initiatives and referendums live in Utah, and in 2025 they dropped it for party candidates' petitions too. In 2026 they required gatherer training, and starting in 2027 many signers will be told whether their signature counted.
