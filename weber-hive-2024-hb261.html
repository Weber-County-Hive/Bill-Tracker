H.B. 261 (2024): Equal Opportunity Initiatives

This 2024 law ended diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) offices and required statements in Utah colleges, public schools and state government. A Weber County lawmaker wrote it. Here is what it does, who voted how, and how it grades.

Short version

  • H.B. 261 was written by Rep. Katy Hall of South Ogden. Sen. Keith Grover carried it in the Senate. Gov. Spencer Cox signed it Jan. 30, 2024, just two weeks into the session.
  • It bars Utah colleges, the public school system and government employers from making people sign or attend things that promote treating people differently, and from using certain personal traits in decisions about jobs or school. It also bars offices that do those things (bill page).
  • It requires the higher education board, the State School Board, the State Auditor and state agency heads to check and report that the rules are followed. Colleges must hire an outside group to run campus climate surveys and send the results to the Legislature.
  • Anyone can file a complaint: with the higher education board for a college, or with the State School Board for public schools.
  • It passed the House 58–14, the Senate 23–6, and the House again 60–14. Reps. Karianne Lisonbee and Ken Ivory voted yes. Rep. Rosemary Lesser of Ogden voted no.
  • Graded by the collective rubric: C+. It adds compliance reports and a complaint path. It changes which programs schools may run, which supporters and opponents see very differently.

Where & who

WhereEvery Utah public college and university, the public school system (districts, charter schools and the State School Board), and government employers, statewide.
WhoRep. Katy Hall (primary sponsor, South Ogden); Sen. Keith Grover (Senate sponsor); the Utah Board of Higher Education, the State School Board and the State Auditor, which check that it is followed.

The bill

H.B. 261 (2024): Equal Opportunity Initiatives LAW

Primary sponsor: Rep. Katy Hall · Senate sponsor: Sen. Keith Grover · Signed Jan. 30, 2024 · Official bill page

In plain words: Colleges, public schools and state offices can’t make anyone sign a diversity statement or go to training that promotes treating people differently. They can’t use things like a person’s race or sex to decide who gets a job or a spot. The state has to check that this is followed, and anyone can complain.

What it does (from the bill’s own words):

  • Bars requiring a person, before or after they are hired or admitted, to submit statements or attend training that promotes differential treatment.
  • Bars using certain personal characteristics in decisions about jobs or education.
  • Bars keeping an office that does those things.
  • Requires the Board of Higher Education, the State School Board, the State Auditor and agency heads to review and report compliance.
  • Requires colleges to hire an outside group for campus climate surveys, and to send the results to the Legislature’s staff, who report to the Education Interim Committee.
  • Sets up a complaint path to the higher education board or the State School Board.
  • Puts money toward a college program (amount not read for this page), with limited exceptions to the bans.

How it connects to other Hive work

Two people in our Who Connects to Whom research voted on this bill: Rep. Karianne Lisonbee (yes) and Rep. Ken Ivory (yes). Rep. Katy Hall, who wrote it, represents part of Weber County.

The 2023 opinion piece: our Education page reports that an April 27, 2023 Deseret News opinion piece, signed by a group that included Jamie Renda’s group and Gloria Vindas, backed H.B. 451 (2023), the earlier DEI bill, which failed in committee. See Who Connects to Whom.

What would make this clearer for the public

These come from gaps in the record, not from either side of the debate.

1. Post the compliance reportsThe law requires compliance reports. A public page with each year’s reports would let anyone check. Who could do it: the boards and the State Auditor.
2. Post the survey summariesThe campus climate survey summaries go to the Education Interim Committee. Posting them with the meeting would make them easy to find. Who could do it: the Legislature.
3. Count the complaintsPost how many complaints were filed and how they were settled. Who could do it: the Board of Higher Education and the State School Board.
