CASE 2026-546 · 2026 GENERAL SESSION

H.B. 546 — Public Lands Duty of Care Amendments

Status: Became law · Signed March 25, 2026

Became Law
Mixed · Leaning Harmful
"Mixed, leaning harmful — useful hazard mapping is combined with an aggressive and legally disputed claim of state jurisdiction over approximately 96% of Utah's federally controlled land."
What It Did

Mapped huge federal landscapes — and claimed jurisdiction over them

Declared a broad state-jurisdiction theory

H.B. 546 invents the term "federal proprietary interest lands" — federally controlled land the bill says the federal government holds only as an ordinary property owner, with no governing or legislative jurisdiction. The law declares that Utah never ceded legislative jurisdiction over these lands, that they cover roughly 35 million acres, and that they make up about 96% of federally controlled land in Utah.

Most significantly: state entities are instructed to presume federally controlled land is merely "federal proprietary interest land" unless the federal government proves otherwise. These are assertions written into state law — passing them doesn't settle their constitutionality or bind the federal government.

Created a statewide mapping project

By January 1, 2028, the Public Lands Policy Coordinating Office must identify and digitally map "landscape-scale lands" — contiguous areas of at least 250,000 acres under common title, lease, or unified management. The map must layer in public roads and access points, potential "landscape public nuisance areas," priority mineral lands, and high-value mineral areas.

Created "landscape public nuisance areas"

The Division of Forestry, Fire and State Lands may designate a public-nuisance area on large forested landscapes, weighing tree density, forest health, insect or disease infestation, wildfire fuel loads, terrain, watershed, weather — and "any other factor the division considers reasonably relevant." That catch-all gives the division substantial discretion.

Mapped mineral-development priorities

The mapping system must also flag lands with federally or state-designated critical minerals, areas near mineral-processing and transport infrastructure, areas with "limited conflicts" with other resources, and areas with high production potential. The office must issue annual recommendations on establishing high-value mineral zones.

What It Did Not Do

The limits of the bill — for now

  • ✕ Transfer ownership of federal land to Utah
  • ✕ Authorize mineral extraction
  • ✕ Approve a particular mine
  • ✕ Authorize logging or vegetation removal
  • ✕ Override a federal land-management plan by itself
  • ✕ Create an immediate enforcement or nuisance-abatement procedure
  • ✕ Establish penalties against a federal agency
  • ✕ Appropriate money for future treatment of mapped land

Calling an area a "landscape public nuisance" on a Utah map does not, by itself, give the state authority to enter federally managed property and alter it.

The Constitutional Conflict

A contested legal premise

The U.S. Constitution's Property Clause gives Congress authority to make rules and regulations concerning property belonging to the United States.

In Kleppe v. New Mexico, the U.S. Supreme Court rejected a narrow reading of that authority — holding that when valid federal public-land legislation conflicts with state law, the state law must give way under the Supremacy Clause. (Cornell Law, full opinion)

Utah may retain generally applicable state authority over matters like water rights, criminal law, and public safety. But H.B. 546 cannot, through a state-law declaration alone, conclusively determine where federal jurisdiction ends. That question may ultimately require federal litigation.

Who It Helped / Who Could Be Harmed

The maps are neutral — how they get used isn't

Who It Helped

  • State officials seeking greater influence over federal-land management
  • The Public Lands Policy Coordinating Office and Federalism Commission
  • Counties seeking additional roads, access, or active forest treatment
  • Communities facing wildfire, insects, disease, or unhealthy forest conditions
  • Mining and mineral-processing interests seeking identification of high-value deposits
  • Grazing, timber, and energy interests favored by Utah's multiple-use policies
  • Emergency-response agencies that could benefit from improved access and hazard information

Who Could Be Harmed

  • Utah taxpayers, if the state's jurisdictional claims produce litigation
  • Conservation interests, if nuisance designations become a basis for roads, logging, or development
  • Wildlife and habitat affected by mineral extraction or vegetation treatment
  • Tribes, if culturally significant landscapes are mapped without meaningful consultation
  • Federal land managers confronted with inconsistent state demands
  • Local communities, if statewide mineral or nuisance designations override local priorities
  • Recreationists, if "access" projects fragment currently roadless areas

The mapping itself doesn't create these effects — but it builds the information and classifications that could support them later.

The Public-Interest Bottom Line

Mapping wildfire hazards, disease, access routes, and mineral resources can support legitimate public planning. But H.B. 546 goes further — it directs state agencies to start from a legal presumption that nearly all federally controlled land in Utah falls under Utah's jurisdiction unless the federal government proves otherwise, then places forest "public nuisances," access routes, and high-value minerals inside the same mapping system.

The bill doesn't authorize Utah to abate those nuisances or develop those minerals today. Its significance is that it builds the factual and legal infrastructure for what may come next.

Will the maps be used primarily to protect communities from wildfire — or to justify roads, logging, mineral development, and expanded state control?

Before Utah acts on these designations, will the public be able to see the data, methodology, private participants, and affected lands behind each map?
