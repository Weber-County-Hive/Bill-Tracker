Utah’s Records Laws, 2022–2026

From 2022 to 2026, Utah lawmakers changed what a records request can cost, who decides appeals, how a person can be cut off from asking, and when you can get your lawyer fees back. Here is who wrote each law, how lawmakers voted, and how each one grades. Two related laws, on lawsuits over public speech and on lawyer fees in general, are included.

Short version

  • Utah’s records law is GRAMA, the Government Records Access and Management Act. It is how anyone asks a government agency for its records. Under GRAMA, a record is public unless a law says otherwise (Utah Code § 63G-2-201(2)).
  • Five laws from 2022 to 2025 changed how that works: H.B. 96 (2022) on request fees, S.B. 231 (2023) on cutting off a “vexatious requester,” H.B. 266 (2024) on mediation and fee-waiver appeals, H.B. 69 (2025) on lawyer fees in records cases, and S.B. 277 (2025), which put one director in place of the seven-member State Records Committee.
  • Two related laws are here too: S.B. 18 (2023), which protects people sued over speaking up in public life, and H.B. 307 (2026), which changed the general rule for lawyer fees in civil cases. H.B. 307 does not change the records law.
  • All seven passed and are law. Every summary on this page was read from the bill’s enrolled text, and every vote from the Legislature’s roll calls.

Where & who

WhereEvery state and local government agency in Utah that answers records requests, and every Utah court that hears records cases or lawsuits over public speech.
WhoSen. Curtis S. Bramble (primary sponsor of S.B. 18 and S.B. 231, Senate sponsor of H.B. 266), Rep. Anthony E. Loubet (primary sponsor of H.B. 266 and H.B. 307, House sponsor of S.B. 231), Rep. Stephanie Gricius (H.B. 69), Rep. Dan N. Johnson (H.B. 96) and Sen. Michael K. McKell (S.B. 277).

The seven laws

H.B. 96 (2022): Government Records Fee Amendments LAW

Primary sponsor: Rep. Dan N. Johnson · Senate sponsor: Sen. Chris H. Wilson · Signed March 22, 2022 · In effect May 4, 2022 · Official bill page · Enrolled text

In plain words: Before this law, nobody could be charged for the first 15 minutes of staff time on a records request. Now an agency can charge for those 15 minutes if you sent it another request in the past 10 days and you are not a “Utah media representative.”

What it does (read from the enrolled bill text):

  • Keeps the first quarter hour of staff time free in general, but lets an agency charge for it when the person sent a separate request in the 10 days before and is not a Utah media representative.
  • Defines “media representative” as someone asking for a record to get information for a story or report published or broadcast to the general public. The definition leaves out a blog, podcast, social media account, or other mass communication open to any member of the public.
  • Keeps the rule that an agency may not charge for reviewing whether a record can be released (with an exception in the law) or for inspecting a record.
  • Checks the law keeps: agencies are still encouraged to fill a request for free when releasing the record mainly benefits the public, and a person who thinks a fee waiver was unreasonably denied can appeal.

S.B. 18 (2023): Public Expression Protection Act LAW

Primary sponsor: Sen. Curtis S. Bramble · House sponsor: Rep. A. Cory Maloy · Signed March 23, 2023 · In effect May 3, 2023 · Session law chapter 488 · Official bill page · Enrolled text

In plain words: If someone sues you over what you said at a public meeting, about something a government body is deciding, or while using your rights to speak, publish, gather or petition, you can ask the judge to end the case early. If the law’s conditions are met, the court must award your costs and lawyer fees.

What it does (read from the enrolled bill text):

  • Creates a fast way to ask a court to end a claim brought against a person for speaking in, or about an issue before, certain government proceedings, or for using certain rights under the U.S. or Utah Constitution.
  • Requires a court to award costs, attorney fees and other litigation expenses in certain circumstances.
  • Repeals Utah’s older Citizen Participation in Government Act.
  • This is a speech law, not a records law. It is here because it covers people who take part in public life and those who report on it.

S.B. 231 (2023): Government Records Access and Management Act Amendments LAW

Primary sponsor: Sen. Curtis S. Bramble · House sponsor: Rep. Anthony E. Loubet · Signed March 23, 2023 · In effect May 3, 2023 · Session law chapter 516 · Official bill page · Enrolled text

In plain words: This created the “vexatious requester” petition. A government agency can ask the state to let it stop filling one person’s records requests for up to a year. It also told agencies to do a reasonable search for records.

What it does (read from the enrolled bill text):

  • Lets a government agency petition the State Records Committee for relief from a “vexatious requester.” (Since May 7, 2025, these petitions go to the director of the Government Records Office instead; Utah Code § 63G-2-209.) If granted, the agency does not have to fill that person’s requests, or requests made for them, for up to one year.
  • At the hearing, discovery is not allowed, but the committee may issue subpoenas. The committee weighs things like how many requests the person made, their scope and language, and any pattern it finds to be abuse or substantial interference with the agency’s work.
  • A request the agency doesn’t have to fill under such an order can’t be appealed.
  • Either side can take the decision to court. The court may award the requester lawyer fees only if the requester substantially wins and the court finds the petition had no merit and no reasonable basis in fact or law. H.B. 69 (2025) later added one more condition: the agency filed the petition in bad faith.
  • Requires every agency to do a “reasonable search” for a requested record, meaning one reasonable in scope and not unreasonably burdensome.
  • The bill’s own summary also says an agency does not have to create a document stating that a requested record does not exist.
  • Checks the law includes: the person gets notice, a written response, a hearing where they can testify and present evidence, a written order with reasons, a one-year limit, and the right to go to court. The decision must weigh the purposes of the records law along with the number and nature of the requests.

H.B. 266 (2024): Government Records Ombudsman Amendments LAW

Primary sponsor: Rep. Anthony E. Loubet · Senate sponsor: Sen. Curtis S. Bramble · Signed March 19, 2024 · In effect May 1, 2024 · Official bill page · Enrolled text

In plain words: If you ask the state’s records ombudsman to help settle a records dispute, your deadlines to appeal and to go to court now stop running while that happens. The appeal steps also now spell out how and when to appeal a refused fee waiver.

What it does (read from the enrolled bill text):

  • Pauses a requester’s deadline to appeal, and the deadline to ask a court for review, from the day the requester asks the government records ombudsman to mediate until the mediation ends as the law sets out.
  • Writes fee-waiver denials into the appeal steps: a requester can appeal one to the agency’s chief administrative officer within 30 days after being told the waiver is denied. (A fee-waiver appeal was already in the law before this bill; H.B. 266 spells out its path and deadline.)
  • Requires a notice that upholds a denial to tell the requester about the right to ask for mediation.
  • Has the ombudsman certify when certain mediations end, or that a side did not agree to mediate.
  • Removes the rule that made the State Records Committee a required party when someone asks a court to review its decision.

H.B. 69 (2025): Government Records and Information Amendments LAW

Primary sponsor: Rep. Stephanie Gricius · Senate sponsor: Sen. Calvin R. Musselman · Signed March 25, 2025 · In effect May 7, 2025 · Official bill page · Enrolled text

In plain words: Going to court over a records denial got more costly for the person asking. To get your lawyer fees back, winning is no longer enough: you must also show the agency acted in bad faith. The bill also limits how officials can use election records.

What it does (read from the enrolled bill text):

  • Legal fees in records cases. A court may make the agency (or another party defending the denial) pay a requester’s lawyer fees only if the requester substantially wins and the court finds that party acted in bad faith.
  • No fees may be awarded to a requester if the case is mainly to benefit the requester’s financial or commercial interest.
  • A court may make a requester pay the agency’s lawyer fees if the agency substantially wins and the court finds the requester acted in bad faith.
  • Adds the same bad-faith test to lawyer fees in vexatious requester cases and when an agency asks a court to keep a record confidential.
  • Election records. A government officer may not use election records except as reasonably related to the officer’s duties and allowed by law, and not for a primarily personal purpose such as a political one. Doing so on purpose is a class B misdemeanor. Officers can still get records the same way any member of the public can.
  • Makes whether a voter put postage on a ballot return envelope private.
  • Checks the law keeps: in deciding fees, a court must still weigh the public benefit of the case, the requester’s interest in the records, and whether the agency had a reasonable basis. A requester can be charged the agency’s fees only on a finding of bad faith.

S.B. 277 (2025): Government Records Management Amendments LAW

In plain words: This replaced the seven-member State Records Committee, which heard records appeals, with one director appointed by the governor with Senate consent.

H.B. 307 (2026): Attorney Fees Amendments LAW

Primary sponsor: Rep. Anthony E. Loubet · Senate sponsor: Sen. Brady Brammer · Signed March 23, 2026 · In effect May 6, 2026 · Official bill page · Enrolled text

In plain words: This changed Utah’s general rule for when a court must make the losing side pay the winner’s lawyer fees. It is a related law: it does not change the records law itself.

What it does (read from the enrolled bill text):

  • Rewrites the general lawyer-fee rule in civil cases (Utah Code § 78B-5-825). A court must award fees to the winner if the losing side’s case or defense was frivolous or had no reasonable basis in law or fact, and was brought to harass, cause unreasonable delay, needlessly raise costs or abuse the court process.
  • Applies the same two-part test to fees spent defending against a motion.
  • Lets a court award no fees or limited fees if the party filed an affidavit of indigency, or if the court states its reason in the record.
  • Does not amend Utah’s records law (GRAMA).

What would make this clearer for the public

These come from gaps in the record, not from either side of any records fight. They would help both agencies and the people who ask.

1. One public count of vexatious requester petitionsHow many petitions agencies file, which agencies, and how each one ended, posted in one place every year. Who could do it: the Government Records Office, or the Legislature by law.
2. Post what records costA yearly total of the fees each agency charged for records requests, and how many fee waivers it denied. Who could do it: each agency, or the Legislature by law.
3. Say who counts as mediaH.B. 96 leaves blogs, podcasts and social media out of its media definition. A plain public explanation of how agencies apply it would help independent reporters know where they stand. Who could do it: the Government Records Office or the Legislature.
4. Report lawyer-fee awards in records casesHow often courts award fees to requesters or agencies since H.B. 69, and how much. Who could do it: the courts or the Legislature.
