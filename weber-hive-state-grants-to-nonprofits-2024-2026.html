State Grants to Private Nonprofits, 2024–2026

Lawmakers can give state money straight to one private group by name. Here are the four bills from 2024 to 2026 about the rules for that money, how lawmakers voted, how each one grades, and how the rules work for one group, Hope of Utah.

Short version

  • Sometimes Utah lawmakers give state money straight to one private group by name. This is called a direct award grant. No other group gets to compete for it.
  • From 2024 to 2026, lawmakers tried four bills about the rules for these grants. Two became law: H.B. 335 (2024) and S.B. 324 (2026). Two died in the last hours of the 2025 session: S.B. 254 and H.B. 553. Both had passed the House with no one voting no.
  • H.B. 335 is the main rule book. A group must show a budget, promise what it will do, send reports, and agree that the state can check its books and take money back. The state may not pay it all at once, and the last payment waits for the final report (H.B. 335 enrolled text).
  • One group under these rules: Hope of Utah. Rep. Karianne Lisonbee asked for money for it two years in a row. In 2025 it got a $150,000 one-time grant. In 2026 lawmakers gave it $187,000 in a line that does not say “one-time,” the label the budget bill uses for money that keeps coming (COBI, 2025; COBI, 2026).
  • Both Hope of Utah grants came out of the state’s Arts & Museums grant budget. The 2025 grant was paid as $135,000 first, with a $15,000 last payment asked for after the spending report. That matches H.B. 335’s rule.
  • Graded by the collective rubric: H.B. 335 B+, S.B. 254 B, H.B. 553 B, S.B. 324 B. None of them gets an A, because none makes these grants compete or puts every grant report online for the public.

Where & who

WhereStatewide. These rules cover every state grant to a nonprofit or private group, in every county, including Weber County.

How a direct award grant works

  1. A lawmaker asks. A lawmaker files a “request for appropriation” naming the group and the amount. The request is posted on the Legislature’s budget site, COBI.
  2. A budget committee hears it. For Hope of Utah, that was the Economic and Community Development budget committee.
  3. The group is named in the budget bill. Under H.B. 335, a direct award grant only counts if the budget bill’s words name the group.
  4. The state signs a contract. The group must give a budget and agree to deliverables, reports, audits and clawback (taking money back).
  5. The state pays in parts. No single lump-sum payment. The last payment waits for the final report.
  6. Anyone can see the payments. Payments show up on Transparent Utah, the state’s spending website.

The four bills

H.B. 335 (2024): State Grant Process Amendments LAW

Primary sponsor: Rep. Val L. Peterson · Senate sponsor: Sen. Jerry W. Stevenson · Signed March 14, 2024 · In effect July 1, 2024 · Official bill page

In plain words: This is the rule book for state grants. A group has to show a budget, say what it will do, send reports and let the state check its books. If it breaks the deal, the state can take the money back.

What it does (from the bill’s own words):

  • Requires a grant recipient to give a proposed budget and agree to deliverables, reporting, audit and clawback rules before it gets any money.
  • Sets how grant money is paid out over time.
  • Requires a review, after a set time, of any grant paid with ongoing money.
  • Sets separate rules for direct award grants and competitive grants. A direct award grant must be named in the budget bill’s own words.
  • Lets the Legislative Auditor General audit any grant.

S.B. 254 (2025): State Grant Revisions DIED

Primary sponsor: Sen. Luz Escamilla · House sponsor: Rep. Melissa G. Ballard · Died March 7, 2025 (pulled back by the Senate and filed at midnight) · Official bill page

In plain words: This would have made lawmakers write the grant’s payment schedule, its promises and its reports right into the budget bill, where anyone can read them. It passed both houses with no one voting no, but the Senate pulled it back on the last night and it died.

What it does (from the bill’s own words):

  • Would have required the budget bill’s words for a direct award grant to include the payment schedule, deliverables, reporting rules and performance measures.

H.B. 553 (2025): State Grant Process Amendments DIED

Primary sponsor: Rep. Val L. Peterson · Senate sponsor: Sen. Jerry W. Stevenson · Died March 7, 2025 (no Senate vote) · Official bill page

In plain words: This would have stopped state agencies from keeping part of a direct award grant for their own office costs, unless lawmakers said they could. It would also have made groups say what other state money they already get. It passed the House with no one voting no, then died in the Senate.

What it does (from the bill’s own words):

  • Would have barred an agency from using direct award grant money for its own administrative costs unless the budget bill allows it.
  • Would have required competitive grant applicants to tell the state about other state money they get.

S.B. 324 (2026): Outcome-based Investment Grant Pilot Framework LAW

Primary sponsor: Sen. Calvin R. Musselman (West Haven) · House sponsor: Rep. Karen M. Peterson · Signed March 18, 2026 · Official bill page

In plain words: This sets up a test. When lawmakers choose, groups that compete for a grant must say ahead of time how they will measure results, and an outside reviewer checks if it worked. It does not cover direct award grants, like Hope of Utah’s, unless lawmakers add them.

What it does (from the bill’s own words):

  • Creates a pilot framework for awarding some grants based on measurable results.
  • Has the Legislative Auditor General set the standards for checking results.
  • Requires applicants in the pilot to file a plan up front and take part in an independent check.

What would make this clearer for the public

These come from gaps in the record. They would help anyone see where grant money goes.

1. Post every grant report onlineH.B. 335 requires reports, but they are not posted. A public page for each grant’s budget, promises and reports would let anyone check. Who could do it: the Legislature or the agency that pays the grant.
2. Bring back S.B. 254It passed both houses with no one voting no. Writing each grant’s promises into the budget bill puts them where the public can read them. Who could do it: any lawmaker, in 2027.
3. Put grants in the right budgetHope of Utah’s request was filed with Public Education but paid from Arts & Museums. Paying a grant from a budget that matches the work makes it easier to follow. Who could do it: the budget committees.
4. Show the review for ongoing grantsWhen H.B. 335’s review of the $187,000 ongoing grant happens, post it. Who could do it: the Legislative Fiscal Analyst.
